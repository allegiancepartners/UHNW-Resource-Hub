Appendix B — Basic Estate Plan

Illustrative New York Family  ·  Allegiance Partners  ·  2026

Overview

A foundational estate plan establishes the legal framework for asset transfer, incapacity management, and wealth preservation. For a family with significant existing wealth, this baseline layer is essential before more complex structures — the Dynasty Trust, grantor trust elections, charitable vehicles — are layered on top. Allegiance recommends engaging a trusts & estates attorney from its Trusted Advisor Bench to complete the Basic Estate Plan.

Core Documents Required

Last Will & Testament
Directs disposition of probate assets; names executor and guardian designations for minor children, if applicable.
Revocable Living Trust
Avoids probate for NY real property; coordinates with the Dynasty Trust and other planning vehicles.
Durable Power of Attorney
Authorizes an agent to manage financial affairs during incapacity.
Healthcare Proxy / HIPAA Authorization
Designates a healthcare decision-maker; HIPAA authorization for medical record access.
Living Will / Advance Directive
Documents end-of-life care preferences. Required under NY Health Care Proxy Law.

NY Estate Tax — The Cliff Effect

New York imposes an estate tax with a distinctive "cliff" phase-out: the state's basic exclusion amount is $7,350,000 for 2026, but for estates between 100% and 105% of that amount, the exclusion is phased out on a sliding scale rather than lost all at once — and above roughly 105% (~$7,717,500), none of the exclusion applies. This produces an unusually punitive effective marginal rate in that band, well above the top statutory rate.
  • NY exemption (2026): $7,350,000
  • Cliff threshold (105% of exemption): ~$7,717,500 — above this, none of the exclusion applies
  • Top rate on amounts above the exemption: 16%
  • Planning action: use the Dynasty Trust to remove assets from the gross estate entirely

Appendix H — Lifetime Gifting & GST Exemption

Annual Exclusion, Lifetime Exemption & Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

The Three Layers of Gift & Transfer Tax Planning

Moving wealth to the next generation efficiently means using three complementary tools in sequence: the annual exclusion (use it or lose it, every year), the lifetime gift/estate exemption (a large, one-time allowance), and the generation-skipping transfer (GST) exemption (a separate allowance that protects gifts to grandchildren and later generations from an additional layer of tax).

Annual Exclusion Gifts

ParameterValue (2026)
Per recipient, per year$19,000
Married couple, gift-splitting$38,000 per recipient
Lifetime exemption usedNone — resets every calendar year
Gift tax return requiredNo, unless gift-splitting is elected

A family with, for example, three children and their spouses, plus six grandchildren, could gift over $200,000 per year to that group under the annual exclusion alone (assuming gift-splitting) — without touching a dollar of lifetime exemption.

Lifetime Gift & Estate Exemption

ParameterValue (2026)
Individual basic exclusion amount$15,000,000
Married couple (portability or gift-splitting)$30,000,000
Unified across gift and estate taxYes — a single lifetime number, used by either mechanism
ReportingForm 709, filed for the calendar year of the gift

Generation-Skipping Transfer (GST) Tax Exemption

The GST tax is a separate, flat 40% tax that applies when assets skip a generation — for example, passing from grandparent directly to grandchild. Each individual also has a GST exemption equal to the same $15,000,000 (2026) as the gift/estate exemption. Allocating GST exemption to the Dynasty Trust on a timely filed Form 709 makes the trust "GST-exempt" — meaning distributions to grandchildren, great-grandchildren, and later generations avoid GST tax entirely, for as long as the trust exists.

Why fund early: allocating exemption against today's contribution locks in GST-exempt status for all future appreciation as well — a $10,000,000 gift today that grows to $60,000,000 over several decades remains entirely GST-exempt, using only $10,000,000 of exemption.

Appendix D — Dynasty Trust

Delaware Dynasty Trust for the Family's Descendants  ·  Illustrative Structure  ·  2026

What Is the Dynasty Trust?

The Dynasty Trust is an irrevocable trust established to hold a portion of the family's wealth — illustrated here at $10,000,000 — for the benefit of the family's descendants (children, current or future, and grandchildren). Delaware situs is recommended for its favorable trust law (Appendix E) and its abolition of the Rule Against Perpetuities, allowing the trust to benefit multiple generations without a forced termination date.

Key Structural Features

  • Situs: Delaware — no state income tax on undistributed trust income; strong privacy laws
  • Irrevocable — assets removed from the family's estate; spendthrift provisions protect from creditors
  • Beneficiaries: the family's descendants — a true multi-generational, dynasty structure
  • Flexible distributions — trustee has discretion over income/principal distributions to beneficiaries
  • Grantor trust option — the family can pay trust income taxes personally, an additional tax-free gift at the family's own rate (Appendix G)

Funding Timing

Funding the trust now, at today's asset values, uses exemption at today's dollar amount — while all future appreciation compounds outside the estate. Funding the same $10,000,000 slice a decade from now, after it has grown, would use far more exemption (or leave far more exposed to estate tax if exemption has run out) than funding it today. Funding early, and allocating GST exemption at the same time (Appendix H), is one of the highest-value planning actions available.

Trustee Selection

  • Independent individual trustee — flexibility, personal relationship, lower cost; succession risk
  • Institutional / corporate trustee (bank trust dept.) — continuity, expertise, regulated; higher cost
  • Directed trust structure — institutional administrative trustee + investment advisor (Allegiance) as advisor
  • Trust Protector — third party with power to modify trust, change trustees, or adapt to law changes

Beneficiary Designations

Beneficiaries: the family's descendants — children and grandchildren, current or future. As a true dynasty structure, the trust is designed to benefit not only the first generation but their descendants as well, taking advantage of Delaware's abolition of the Rule Against Perpetuities.

Action: confirm structure and drafting with T&E counsel before funding.

Appendix E — Trust Situs

Delaware as Optimal Trust Jurisdiction  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

Why Delaware?

Trust situs (legal jurisdiction) determines which state's laws govern the trust and whether undistributed trust income is subject to state income tax. Delaware offers the most favorable combination of tax treatment, legal flexibility, privacy, and duration for the Dynasty Trust.

Delaware Advantages

No State Income Tax
DE does not tax undistributed income of a trust with no DE-resident beneficiaries. The Dynasty Trust avoids both NY State (10.9%) and NYC (3.876%, where applicable) income tax at the trust level.
No Rule Against Perpetuities
DE abolished the RAP in 1995. Trusts can last for a very long duration. New York, by contrast, has an 85-year RAP.
Directed Trust Statute
DE allows bifurcation of trustee duties — separate investment advisor (Allegiance) and administrative trustee. Maximizes flexibility and professional management.
Asset Protection
Strong spendthrift and creditor protection provisions. Trust assets insulated from beneficiary creditors, divorce claims, and judgments.
Privacy
DE court records relating to trusts are not public. No public registration of trust terms required.
Legal Sophistication
DE has the most developed body of trust law; Delaware Court of Chancery provides expert trust dispute resolution.

Appendix G — Grantor vs. Non-Grantor Trusts

Income Tax Treatment  ·  SLAT & SLANT as Examples  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

Grantor vs. Non-Grantor Trust — Income Tax Treatment

Every irrevocable trust is classified as either a Grantor Trust or a Non-Grantor Trust for income tax purposes. This classification determines who pays the income tax on trust earnings — and has profound implications for how quickly the trust corpus compounds (see Appendix M).

Grantor Trust
The family pays income tax on ALL trust income personally, at their own rate. The trust itself pays no tax. Each tax payment is effectively an additional tax-free gift to the trust — supercharging growth. The trust corpus compounds at the full gross return.
Non-Grantor Trust
The trust is a separate taxpayer. It pays its own income tax on earnings at trust tax rates. The trust corpus compounds at the net after-tax return. No additional depletion of the family's personal estate from tax payments.
The Trade-Off
Grantor Trust: faster corpus growth, additional estate depletion via tax payments — best for families with outside liquidity to absorb the tax bill. Non-Grantor: simpler, self-contained, still outside the estate. See Appendix M for the 29-year consolidated family wealth comparison.

SLAT & SLANT — Spousal Trust Examples

SLATs and SLANTs are irrevocable trusts that include a spouse as a lifetime beneficiary, offering estate-tax removal while preserving indirect access to the assets through the spouse.

SLAT — Spousal Lifetime Access Trust
Irrevocable trust; grantor spouse transfers assets; beneficiary spouse has discretionary access. Because the beneficiary spouse can benefit, the trust is treated as a grantor trust for income tax purposes.
SLANT — Spousal Lifetime Access Non-Grantor Trust
A SLAT drafted so that an "adverse party's" consent is required for distributions — breaking grantor trust status, so the trust becomes its own separate taxpayer instead.

Appendix I — Grantor Retained Annuity Trusts (GRATs)

Transferring Appreciation at Little to No Gift Tax Cost  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

What Is a GRAT?

A Grantor Retained Annuity Trust is an irrevocable trust into which a grantor contributes assets and, in return, receives a fixed annuity payment for a set term (often 2–10 years). At the end of the term, whatever remains in the trust — including all appreciation above the IRS's assumed interest rate (the §7520 rate) — passes to the named beneficiaries with little or no gift tax cost.

Why It Works Well for Volatile or Concentrated Assets

  • A "zeroed-out" GRAT can be structured so the annuity payments return the full value contributed (plus the §7520 rate) — meaning the taxable gift at funding is close to $0
  • Any growth above the §7520 rate passes to beneficiaries gift-tax-free at the end of the term
  • Well suited to a single concentrated, volatile position — a family can run several short-term GRATs in sequence ("rolling GRATs") to capture upside while limiting downside if a GRAT underperforms and simply terminates with nothing extra passing
  • If the grantor dies during the GRAT term, the remaining trust assets are pulled back into the taxable estate — a mortality risk best evaluated with T&E counsel

GRAT vs. Dynasty Trust — Different Tools for Different Assets

Appendix J — Charitable Giving Vehicles

DAF, CRUT, Foundation  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

Overview

Charitable giving is both a values-based decision and a planning opportunity. Vehicles range from simple (DAF) to sophisticated (private foundation), each with different control, income, and deduction characteristics.

Donor Advised Fund (DAF)

  • Simplest vehicle — contribution is irrevocable; grants recommended over time
  • Deduction: FMV of assets contributed; avoids capital gains on appreciated stock or securities
  • Minimum contribution: $5,000 (Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, others)
  • No annual payout requirement — flexible grant timing
  • Best for: an immediate deduction plus ongoing, flexible philanthropy

Charitable Remainder Unitrust (CRUT)

  • Contribute appreciated stock or other assets — no immediate capital gains tax
  • Trust pays out a fixed % (commonly ~5%) of corpus annually to the family for life or a term of years
  • Remainder to charity at termination — partially deductible at contribution
  • Creates a diversified income stream from a concentrated appreciated position
  • Best for: income need + charitable intent + a large appreciated position

Private Foundation

  • Full control over grant-making; family members can serve as officers
  • Deduction limited to 30% AGI (cash) / 20% AGI (appreciated property)
  • 5% annual distribution requirement; excise taxes on investment income
  • Best for: significant philanthropic intent, family legacy, and multi-generational grant-making control
  • Higher administrative cost; subject to self-dealing rules
A common pattern: establish a DAF for straightforward, flexible giving, and consider a CRUT where a large appreciated position also needs to generate income. A private foundation is typically reserved for families with a significant, ongoing philanthropic commitment and a desire for hands-on grant-making control across generations.

Appendix K — Private Placement Life Insurance (PPLI)

Tax-Exempt Growth Inside a Life Insurance Wrapper  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

What Is PPLI?

Private Placement Life Insurance is a variable life insurance policy, customized for high-net-worth investors, that wraps an investment portfolio in a life insurance contract. Because the policy is life insurance under the tax code, investment growth inside it is not subject to annual income tax, and death benefits pass to beneficiaries income-tax-free.

Where It Fits Alongside a Dynasty Trust

  • A PPLI policy can be owned by the Dynasty Trust itself — combining the trust's estate-tax removal with the policy's income-tax-free growth
  • Particularly effective for tax-inefficient investment strategies (frequent trading, hedge-fund-style strategies) that would otherwise generate significant annual taxable income
  • Best suited to families who have already used core trust and gifting strategies and are looking for an additional, more advanced layer
  • Requires meaningful minimum premium levels and ongoing policy administration — not a first step, but a later-stage option

Key Considerations

FeatureDetail
Income tax on growthNone, while held inside the policy
Death benefitPasses income-tax-free to beneficiaries
Estate taxRemoved from the estate only if properly owned outside the insured's estate (e.g., by an irrevocable trust)

What Is an Estate Freeze — and Why Does It Matter?

An estate freeze transfers appreciating assets into the Dynasty Trust today, so that all future growth accrues outside the taxable estate. The question is not just "how big does the trust get?" — it is "how much does the family receive in total, after estate tax, compared to doing nothing?" The three scenarios below compare all assets on a consolidated basis from the same starting point.

Same starting capital in all cases: personal assets held outside any trust $20,000,000 + Dynasty Trust $10,000,000 = $30,000,000. Age today: 55 · Life expectancy: 84 (SSA actuarial default, female) · 29 years of compounding.

Gross return: 6.5% · Combined income tax rate: 32.0% · Net after-tax: ~4.42% · Grantor Trust: 6.5% (full gross) · Estate tax: Federal 40% (above $15M exemption) + NY 16% (cliff triggered on estates this size) — figures mirror the interactive Estate Freeze calculator in the Wealth Transfer Toolkit at its default settings.
