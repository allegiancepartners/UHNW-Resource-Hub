Appendix A — Basic Estate Plan

Overview

A foundational estate plan establishes the legal framework for asset transfer, incapacity management, and wealth preservation. Before any freeze, gifting, or trust structure is layered on — the subject of Appendices B onward — this baseline layer needs to be in place. Allegiance recommends engaging a trusts & estates attorney from its Trusted Advisor Bench to complete or refresh the Basic Estate Plan.

Core Documents Required

Reciprocal Wills
Directs disposition of probate assets, names an executor, and designates a guardian for any minor children.
Revocable Living Trust
Avoids probate for real property held in New York and elsewhere; coordinates with any freeze/dynasty trust and other assets.
Durable Power of Attorney
Each spouse names the other as primary agent, with a successor agent to manage financial affairs during incapacity.
Health Care Proxy / Advance Directive
Each spouse designates the other as healthcare decision-maker, combined with end-of-life care preferences, plus HIPAA authorization for medical record access.

New York Estate & Inheritance Tax

New York imposes its own estate tax, separate from the federal tax — current working exemption ≈ $7.2M per individual (not portable between spouses). ⚠ Reconfirm current-year exemption with counsel. NY's exemption is not portable and its cliff design taxes the entire estate — not just the excess — once the estate exceeds ~105% of the exemption. Modeled here as a linear phase-in to that effect, not the exact statutory schedule.
NY's exemption is a cliff, not a phase-out: an estate at or below the exemption owes nothing, but once the estate exceeds roughly 105% of the exemption, New York taxes the entire estate — not just the excess. An estate landing just inside that band is one of the most expensive places to be, dollar for dollar, in NY estate planning; lifetime gifting (Appendix E) that brings a taxable estate back under the exemption is disproportionately valuable here.
New York has no separate inheritance tax by beneficiary class (unlike New Jersey) — its estate tax applies based on estate size regardless of who inherits.

Appendix C — Grantor vs. Non-Grantor Trusts

Income Tax Treatment  ·  New York Blended Rate: 38.6%

Income Tax Treatment

Every irrevocable trust is classified as either a Grantor Trust or a Non-Grantor Trust for income tax purposes. This determines who pays the income tax on trust earnings — and has profound implications for how quickly the trust corpus compounds (see Appendix E).

Grantor Trust
Eleanor Whitfield pays income tax on ALL trust income personally, at a 38.6% blended rate. The trust itself pays no tax. Each tax payment is effectively an additional tax-free gift to the trust — supercharging growth.
Non-Grantor Trust
The trust is a separate taxpayer, paying its own income tax at a 38.6% blended rate on its earnings. No additional depletion of the personal estate from tax payments.
The Trade-Off
Grantor Trust: faster corpus growth, additional personal-estate depletion via tax payments — best with outside liquidity to absorb the tax bill. Non-Grantor: simpler, self-contained, still outside the estate.
Recommendation: elect Grantor Trust status if Eleanor Whitfield has sufficient outside liquidity to comfortably cover the trust's annual tax bill — it meaningfully increases the total amount ultimately passing to descendants (see Appendix E). If liquidity is tight, Non-Grantor is the simpler, still clearly positive default.

Appendix D — Charitable Giving Vehicles

Planned Vehicle: DAF+PF  ·  Funding: $2.0M

Overview

A concentrated, appreciated position or a high-income year is an optimal moment to establish or fund a charitable giving strategy. Vehicles range from simple (DAF) to sophisticated (private foundation).

Donor Advised Fund (DAF)

  • Simplest vehicle — contribution is irrevocable; grants recommended over time
  • Deduction: FMV of assets contributed; avoids capital gains on appreciated securities
  • No annual payout requirement — flexible grant timing

Charitable Remainder Unitrust (CRUT)

  • Contribute appreciated securities — no immediate capital gains tax
  • Trust pays a fixed % of corpus annually for life or a term of years; remainder to charity
  • Best for: income need + charitable intent + a large concentrated position

Private Foundation

  • Full control over grant-making; family members can serve as officers
  • 5% annual distribution requirement; excise taxes on investment income
  • Best for: significant philanthropic intent ($5M+), family legacy, grant-making control
Current plan: DAF+PF, funded at $2.0M — existing vehicle: Whitfield Family DAF.

Appendix F — Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT)

Zeroed-Out Structure  ·  10-Year Term  ·  §7520 Rate 5.0%

How a Zeroed-Out GRAT Works

Eleanor Whitfield contributes $10.0M to an irrevocable trust in exchange for a fixed annuity paid back over 10 years. The annuity is sized so its actuarial value — discounted at the IRS §7520 rate (5.0%, the rate in effect for the month of funding) — equals the amount contributed. That "zeroes out" the taxable gift: little to no exemption is used to fund the GRAT at all.

Anything the trust earns above the §7520 rate passes to the remainder beneficiaries — typically a dynasty trust for descendants — completely free of gift and estate tax. If the trust underperforms the hurdle rate, the annuity simply returns the full contribution (plus the hurdle return) to Eleanor Whitfield, and the technique costs nothing beyond legal fees.

GRATs work best with a short term (2–3 years is common) on an asset expected to meaningfully outperform the §7520 rate, and with enough independent liquidity to make the annuity payments without selling the underlying asset. A "rolling GRAT" strategy — using each year's annuity payment to fund a new short-term GRAT — can be layered on to keep the leverage going.
Mortality risk: if Eleanor Whitfield does not survive the GRAT term, some or all of the trust reverts to the taxable estate, unwinding the benefit. Term length should be chosen with this risk explicitly in mind.

Appendix G — Sale to a Defective Grantor Trust (IDGT)

Installment-Note Sale  ·  10-Year Note  ·  AFR 4.5%

How the Technique Works

Eleanor Whitfield first makes a modest seed gift — $1.0M, roughly 10% of the asset's value — to a trust that is "defective" for income tax purposes (a grantor trust) but effective for estate tax purposes (assets are out of the taxable estate). Eleanor Whitfield then sells an asset worth $10.0M to that trust in exchange for an interest-only promissory note at the Applicable Federal Rate (4.5% for a 10-year term), with the principal due as a balloon payment at maturity.

Because the sale is between a grantor and their own grantor trust, it is a non-event for income tax purposes — no gain is recognized. Because it's a sale for full fair value (not a gift), it uses almost no exemption — only the initial seed gift does. Any growth in the trust above the note's interest rate compounds for the beneficiaries, transfer-tax-free.

Appendix H — Valuation Discounting via Family Entities

Minority-Interest & Marketability Discounts  ·  Eleanor Whitfield

Why a $1 Interest Isn't Always Valued at $1

When a business or real estate interest is held through a family LLC or family limited partnership (FLP) and a non-controlling, non-marketable interest is gifted — rather than the underlying asset itself — the IRS accepts that a willing buyer would pay less for that interest than its pro-rata share of the entity's net asset value. Two discounts typically combine: a minority-interest discount (the interest can't control distributions, sale, or liquidation) and a marketability discount (there is no ready market for a non-controlling interest in a family entity).

Illustrative Numbers

Value
Pro-rata (undiscounted) value of the interest gifted$10.0M
Combined discount applied30%
Value for gift/exemption reporting$7.0M
Exemption preserved by the discount$3.0M
The full economic interest — $10.0M of underlying value — still moves to the trust; only the amount counted against Eleanor Whitfield's lifetime exemption is reduced. This is the single highest-leverage step available for a business or real estate archetype and should always be considered before an outright gift of a controlling or directly-held interest.
Discounts must be supported by a qualified independent appraisal at the time of the gift — a round, unsupported percentage invites an IRS challenge. Coordinate the entity's operating agreement, the appraisal, and the gift's timing closely with counsel.

Appendix I — Qualified Personal Residence Trust (QPRT)

10-Year Term  ·  §7520 Rate 5.0%

How a QPRT Works

Eleanor Whitfield transfers a residence worth $6.0M into an irrevocable trust while retaining the right to live in it, rent-free, for a fixed term of 10 years. Because Eleanor Whitfield keeps that right, the taxable gift is only the present value of the remainder interest — discounted at the §7520 rate — not the full value of the house.

If Eleanor Whitfield survives the full term, the residence passes to the remainder beneficiaries (typically in further trust for descendants) at the gift-tax value locked in on day one, regardless of how much the house has appreciated since. If Eleanor Whitfield does not survive the term, the residence is pulled back into the taxable estate and the technique produces no benefit — this is a binary risk, not a partial one, and term length should be chosen conservatively relative to health and life expectancy.

Appendix K — Basis Planning & Step-Up Considerations

Eleanor Whitfield  ·  Step-Up Status: Partial

Two Very Different Outcomes for the Same Asset

An asset gifted during lifetime carries over the donor's original cost basis to the recipient — any embedded gain is preserved and taxed on a future sale. The same asset, held until death, generally gets a step-up in basis to its fair market value on the date of death, permanently erasing the embedded gain for income tax purposes. This is the single most common reason to hold a highly appreciated asset rather than gift it, even when gifting would save estate tax.

The trade-off in one sentence: gifting saves estate tax (40% federal, plus any state layer) on future growth, but forgoes a basis step-up (up to 23.8% federal, plus state, on the embedded gain) that holding until death would otherwise provide. For an asset with a large embedded gain and a client not expecting to sell it soon, the math can favor holding more often than intuition suggests.

Inherited-Asset Basis Status

Basis step-up on assets already inherited: Partial. Retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s) are a notable exception — as income in respect of a decedent (IRD), they never receive a basis step-up regardless of how they pass, and carry their own embedded income tax liability for whoever ultimately withdraws the funds.

Basis history should be confirmed asset-by-asset with the client's CPA before any gifting decision — a blanket "gift everything to the trust now" instruction can inadvertently convert a step-up opportunity into a permanent embedded gain.
